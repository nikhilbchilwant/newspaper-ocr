Latest Telegrams.

The Sikkim Expedition.

(From our own correspondent.)

Allahabad, March 22.

A telegram dated Camp Sedonchen, 20th March, says: To-day we have actually come to close quarters with the Thibetans. After Colonel Graham had found by his reconnaissance of yesterday that a hostile force was barring the road, he took extra precautions against a night attack, but the enemy did not venture out.

This morning a small force, consisting of 100 men of the Derbyshire Regiment, under Captain Wylly, and 100 Pioneers, under Colonel Bromhead, moved up the steep hill road towards the stockade, while Major Keith, with his two screw-guns, followed an hour later. It was a hard climb, but the troops were cheered by finding that the Thibetans had not deserted their position.

During the night the enemy had chosen an excellent point on the road to oppose us. On a steep wooded knoll they had built a stockade, some twenty yards long. The Pioneers went at the stockade in gallant style. The Thibetans were holding the position with about 40 men in support, some few hundred yards in the rear.

They fired from matchlocks and shot arrows, supposed to be poisoned. They were cleared out of the stockade at the point of the bayonet, and the detachment of the Derbyshire then took the barricade on the road in flank from above.

Another despatch, dated Pedong, 22nd March, says the Thibetans held on stubbornly even after the stockade was taken, their leader showing much bravery. He was shot dead, and his men beat a hasty retreat. Their loss is estimated at twenty killed and wounded. Our casualties were very few, owing to the rapid rush made by the troops, who fired only some two hundred rounds altogether.

Captain Lumsden, who advanced to the break in the road, was hit by a bullet in the arm, the ball passing through the flesh but not touching the bone. Colonel Bromhead was hit by a stone; three or four sepoys suffered contusions. A prisoner stated that the stockade was built two days ago.

The garrison of Lingtu was 200 strong, but 300 men were expected to arrive the next day, and 800 more were on their way from Chumbi. Colonel Graham pushed on as far as Garni, three miles farther on and 10,700 feet above the sea. Snow lay along the road, and the cold was severe. No further sign of the enemy was seen, but tracks of wounded men could be followed by blood marks on the rocks.

The Thibetans left their tents standing and threw away their clothing and supplies along the road. Colonel Bromhead, with 150 Pioneers, held Garni that night; 100 of the Derbyshire, under Captain Wylly, with Major Keith’s two guns, remained at Jeluk, below the stockade. The force would move against Lingtu early the following morning.

General Summary

…standing camps for young and weakly soldiers at Dalhousie, Ranikhet and Chakrata.

The Madras Diocesan Conference, recently sitting at Madras, resolved on the appointment of a permanent Diocesan Council. The Duke of Orleans had not gone with the Sikkim expedition, but was still shooting in the Terai. Mr. Jamsetjee Nusserwanjee Petit, a well-known Parsee resident of Bombay, died on Monday. A new Masonic Hall had been opened at Calcutta. H.M.S. Bacchante arrived in Bombay from Colombo on Monday.

Obituary. Captain D. Stewart, 2nd Battalion Seaforth Highlanders, at Bareilly.

General Summary

At the hour of writing it is almost certain that the Sikkim expeditionary force has reached, stormed and taken possession of Lingtu Fort, which the Thibetan intruders, though repeatedly warned to evacuate, had continued to hold. The last day of grace allowed them was Thursday the 15th; until then the British force remained quietly encamped at Pedong, so near Lingtu that the fort could be seen with field-glasses in the early morning before clouds covered the hills.

On the eve of the final advance, a tardy message came from the Rajah of Sikkim, asking for delay so that he might mediate with the Thibetans. The hour for such temporising had passed: ample time had been allowed for the friendly intervention he now professed himself willing to make. The order to advance was given at nine o’clock on Friday morning, the 16th.

The column comprised 100 men of the Derbyshire Regiment and 300 of the 32nd Pioneers, with two mountain guns under Major Keith, all commanded by Colonel Graham. A reserve of equal strength remained at Pedong. The men set out in excellent health and spirits, with several days of arduous marching ahead.

The distance was short in a direct line, but the zig-zag route was a roughly cleared track through jungle, repeatedly doubling back, climbing and descending steep hillsides. Swollen streams, difficult transport, fever in the valleys, snow and frost on the heights, and a violent thunderstorm at the outset all impeded progress. The first day’s march covered only eight miles, including a descent of 2,700 feet and a climb of more than 5,000 feet. The transport mules performed their trying work admirably.

On Monday the 19th the foot of the ridge on which Lingtu Fort stands was reached. The ascent ahead rose roughly 5,000 feet in five miles. A reconnaissance party went out that afternoon; after about two miles, the men encountered a road obstructed by large stones and felled trees, with a stockade farther on.

(General Summary, continued.)

The Thibetan outpost numbered about fifty and appeared to be armed with matchlocks; one man had a rifle. On the 20th a company of the Derbyshire Regiment and a company of Pioneers, with the two guns, advanced to clear the stockade. The remainder of the column stayed in camp, since a general advance was impossible until the road had been improved. At the hour of writing no further particulars were available.

Our men were now in an extremely cold climate: Lingtu is 12,617 feet above sea level. The Thibetan force there was said to number 200 so-called regulars and 600 or 700 village levies. The fort was described as a single rough-stone wall, about eight feet thick and sixteen feet high, built across the road to the Jeylapla Pass and Thibet. At either end stood a tower or bastion; a stone hut behind the wall offered further shelter. The road passed through a gateway, with two small sungars protecting the zig-zag approach. The wall was loopholed, and much of the garrison was believed to have guns or rifles.

From the standpoint of assault, the fort did not appear difficult to capture. But experience in Upper Burmah showed how serious casualties might result from ambuscades and shots from the jungle. Rumours that Thibetans and unruly inhabitants of Sikkim would harass the advance led to orders warning the soldiers not to drink water at casual places, lest it be poisoned. The Rajah remained at Guntak, apparently waiting to see how events developed, since the British operations were directed against the Thibetan intruders, not against him.

Telegraphy, the paper observed, was changing social and political life, notably at moments of international rejoicing or sorrow. The Queen’s Jubilee was an example of the former; the death of the Emperor William, of the latter. Though printed accounts of the Emperor’s death had yet to arrive, telegraph cables had enabled German and English communities around the world to express their grief simultaneously.

The funeral service in Berlin was touching in its simplicity. Prince Bismarck, Count von Moltke and the new Emperor Frederick III, men closely associated with the late Emperor, were unable to attend, overcome by strain and sorrow. In London, the service was held at Westminster Abbey, and the Queen was present. Similar services were held throughout the English colonies and, it was thought, the German colonies too.

In Bombay the service at the Cathedral was especially impressive. The building was densely crowded; the north transept was allotted to the German community, and the German Consul occupied a prominent place in the procession. The Duchess of Connaught, in deep mourning, was escorted by the Commander-in-Chief and Lord and Lady Reay. Members of the Council, High Court judges, Army and Navy officers, civil officials and sixteen foreign consuls attended. The service, chiefly musical, was conducted by the Bishop of Bombay and the Cathedral chaplain.

(The Cathedral service continued.)

A mourning salute of ninety-one guns—the number of years in the late Emperor’s life—was fired at the chief military stations of the Empire.

Police Sepoy Acquitted

The public, the press and the police could claim a well-merited triumph in the High Court’s reversal of Mr. Crawley-Boevey’s severe sentence on the police sepoy Bhawoo Jivajee. Sentenced to four months’ rigorous imprisonment, he had been released on bail; Justices Birdwood and Parsons acquitted him and ordered his release. The paper had argued that he acted in good faith on his superior officers’ orders, and the judges found that he had reasonable grounds for doing so. Bombay’s police had subscribed Rs. 1,300 to free their comrade. The editorial suggested that Government might reimburse them, and said the decision preserved established police practice.

The Salt Tax

The Bombay Presidency Association had sent Government of India a strong protest against increasing the salt tax. Its memorial argued that the burden would fall on poor rural and labouring people, already contributing heavily from small incomes. The paper contrasted this with Sir Dinshaw Manockjee Petit’s statement in the Supreme Legislative Council that an additional duty of eight annas per maund would scarcely affect the masses. Petit remained President of the Association despite the opposing positions, prompting the editorial to ask whether its members would seek a resolution.

Judicial Appointments

The paper questioned an appointment to a second-grade judgeship, seeing it as evidence that Government might recruit senior judicial officers from the Revenue Department. The appointee had previously returned to Revenue service after withdrawing an earlier election to enter the Judicial Department, then re-entered the judicial branch in 1885. The editorial asked what such an appointment meant for the principle of separating Revenue and Judicial branches.
